Chapter 1: Electric Current

Section 1.1 Charge and current
Electric current is the rate at which electric charge passes a point in a circuit. The relationship is I = Q / t, where I is current, Q is charge, and t is time. Charge is measured in coulombs and time in seconds. One ampere means that one coulomb of charge passes a point each second. If 120 coulombs pass a point in 60 seconds, the current is 2 amperes. Conventional current is defined in the direction of positive charge flow; in a metal wire, electrons drift in the opposite direction.

Section 1.2 Measuring current
The SI unit of electric current is the ampere, represented by A. An ammeter measures electric current and is connected in series with the component being measured, so the same current passes through both. An ideal ammeter has zero resistance; a practical ammeter has very low resistance to avoid significantly changing the circuit current. Connecting an ammeter directly across a cell is unsafe because its very low resistance can allow a large current. A closed conducting path is needed for a sustained current in a simple battery circuit.

Chapter 2: Potential Difference and Resistance

Section 2.1 Potential difference
Potential difference is the work done per unit charge when charge moves between two points. The relationship is V = W / Q, where V is potential difference, W is work done, and Q is charge. One volt means one joule of work per coulomb of charge. A voltmeter measures the potential difference between two points and is connected in parallel across the component. An ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance; a practical voltmeter has very high resistance so that it draws very little current.

Section 2.2 Ohm's law
For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, the current is directly proportional to the potential difference across it. Ohm's law is V = I x R, where R is resistance. Resistance is measured in ohms. A resistor with 6 volts across it and a current of 2 amperes has a resistance of 3 ohms. If the voltage across a fixed resistance is doubled while temperature remains constant, the current doubles. If resistance is doubled while voltage is kept fixed, the current is halved. A graph of V against I for an ohmic conductor is a straight line through the origin, and its slope equals the resistance.

Section 2.3 Factors affecting resistance
For a uniform wire, resistance is given by R = resistivity x length / cross-sectional area. At constant temperature and for the same material, a longer wire has greater resistance, while a thicker wire has lower resistance. Doubling the length doubles the resistance if the cross-sectional area stays the same. Doubling the cross-sectional area halves the resistance if the length stays the same. Resistivity describes the material rather than the dimensions of a particular wire. Copper has low resistivity and is commonly used for connecting wires.

Chapter 3: Series and Parallel Circuits

Section 3.1 Resistors in series
Resistors in series are connected one after another in a single conducting path. The same current passes through every resistor in series. The total potential difference is the sum of the potential differences across the individual resistors. The equivalent resistance is R_total = R_1 + R_2. Resistors of 2 ohms and 4 ohms in series have an equivalent resistance of 6 ohms. Connecting more positive resistances in series increases the equivalent resistance and reduces the current when the supply voltage stays fixed.

Section 3.2 Resistors in parallel
Resistors in parallel are connected across the same two points, creating separate conducting branches. Each branch has the same potential difference across it. The total current is the sum of the branch currents. For two parallel resistors, 1 / R_total = 1 / R_1 + 1 / R_2. Two resistors of 6 ohms each in parallel have an equivalent resistance of 3 ohms. The equivalent resistance of positive resistances in parallel is less than the smallest individual resistance. Adding another parallel branch lowers the equivalent resistance.

Section 3.3 Independent branches
Parallel branches provide separate paths for current. If one branch is opened, current can still flow through the other complete branches. In a series circuit, an open anywhere in the single path stops current throughout that path. Household appliances are connected in parallel so that each appliance receives the supply voltage and can be switched independently. If one appliance is switched off, the other parallel appliances can continue to operate, provided their branches and supply remain intact.

Chapter 4: Electrical Power and Energy

Section 4.1 Electrical power
Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or converted. Power is measured in watts, and one watt equals one joule per second. The relationship is P = V x I. A component with 12 volts across it and a current of 2 amperes uses 24 watts. For a resistor obeying Ohm's law, power can also be written as P = I^2 x R or P = V^2 / R. At a fixed voltage, a lower resistance draws more current and uses more power. One kilowatt equals 1000 watts.

Section 4.2 Electrical energy
For a device running at constant power, electrical energy transferred is E = P x t. Using power in watts and time in seconds gives energy in joules. Using power in kilowatts and time in hours gives energy in kilowatt-hours. A kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, not a unit of power. One kilowatt-hour equals 3,600,000 joules. A 1 kilowatt heater operating for 2 hours transfers 2 kilowatt-hours of energy, equal to 7,200,000 joules. At constant power, doubling the operating time doubles the energy transferred.
